Flagship AI / Finance Project

SEC Filing NLP

A research project on whether the language inside annual filings can be structured, scored, and tested in a disciplined way. The goal was not to claim a magic market signal. It was to build a careful pipeline and understand where the signal was useful, noisy, or fragile.

Factor extraction

Making filings easier to compare.

The extraction layer grouped filing language into interpretable factor families: liquidity, regulatory exposure, demand uncertainty, cost pressure, competitive risk, and management tone. I cared less about producing a single clever score and more about building features that could be inspected.

That inspection step mattered. Some phrases were genuinely informative; others were standard legal language. The pipeline had to make room for both quantitative scoring and human review.

Sentiment scoring

Tone is useful only when it is grounded.

I treated sentiment as one lens rather than the final answer. The scoring layer looked at negative, positive, and uncertainty-oriented language, then compared those signals by section and factor type.

The most useful patterns came from shifts in specific language, not broad labels like positive or negative. A filing can sound cautious for good reasons, and a confident filing can still hide risk.

RL alignment

Rewarding signals that behaved sensibly.

The alignment layer was designed to discourage the model from chasing noisy patterns. Reward ideas included consistency across related sections, interpretability of extracted factors, and performance that did not collapse outside the training window.

This part of the project was useful because it forced a sharper question: is the model learning filing language, or is it learning accidental quirks in the sample?

Key findings

What the project taught me.

Specificity beats volume. More text did not automatically mean better signal. The most useful features were tied to interpretable sections and factor families.

Uncertainty language needs context. Caution can be a risk signal, but it can also reflect responsible disclosure.

Finance models need humility. A backtest is a research tool, not a guarantee. The strongest work was in making assumptions visible.

Lessons learned

The hard part was not the model.

The hard part was deciding what counted as evidence. I learned to pay close attention to baselines, leakage, filing boilerplate, and the difference between an interesting pattern and a reliable one.

That lesson carries into the rest of my portfolio: good data science is not just building something complex. It is knowing what the work can and cannot say.
